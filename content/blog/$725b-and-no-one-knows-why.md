In the first quarter of 2026, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta reported more than \$130 billion in capital expenditures in a single quarter. Their combined full-year projections are staggering: Amazon at \$200 billion, Microsoft at \$190 billion, Alphabet at \$175 to \$185 billion, Meta at \$125 to \$145 billion, and Oracle at \$50 billion. Add it up and you're looking at somewhere between \$700 and \$725 billion in 2026 alone, with Bank of America and Evercore already projecting the number will cross \$1 trillion in 2027.
Private equity is piling in too, financing purpose-built data centers and power plants. Morgan Stanley estimates that tech companies will spend \$2.9 trillion on chips, servers, and data center infrastructure between 2025 and 2028. Goldman Sachs projects \$765 billion in annual AI capex in 2026, growing to \$1.6 trillion by 2031.
But here's the thing nobody wants to say out loud: the revenue to justify this spending doesn't exist yet. This is either the greatest infrastructure bet in history or the most expensive act of faith since the dot-com fiber buildout.
## The raw numbers are hard to process
Two years ago, combined hyperscaler capex was roughly \$200 billion. In 2025, it hit \$400 billion. Now we're approaching \$725 billion. That's not a gradual ramp. That's a vertical line.
To put it in perspective, this spending exceeds the railroad boom, the interstate highway system, and the Apollo space program. AI-related capital expenditures have surpassed the U.S. consumer as the primary driver of economic growth, accounting for 1.1% of GDP growth in the first half of 2025 according to JP Morgan estimates. AI-related stocks have driven 75% of S&P 500 returns, 80% of earnings growth, and 90% of capital spending growth since ChatGPT launched in November 2022.
Every hyperscaler tells the same story: they're supply-constrained, not demand-constrained. Microsoft says it could have grown Azure faster if it had more data center capacity. Meta's CFO Susan Li admits the company has "continued to underestimate our compute needs." Satya Nadella says the company is investing so it can handle "exponential" improvements in AI systems.
But investors aren't fully buying it. Shares of Google, Amazon, Microsoft, and Meta all sold off following their Q1 2026 earnings calls. The Times branded the combined spend the "greatest capital misallocation in history." Meta's stock dropped 8% despite revenue growth, with Jefferies analysts writing that the company "likely remains in the penalty box pending clearer capex ROI."
## We've seen this movie before
The late 1990s telecom bubble is the closest historical parallel, and it's both reassuring and terrifying depending on which part of the story you focus on.
During the telecom boom, companies poured hundreds of billions of dollars into fiber optic networks, convinced that bandwidth demand would grow forever. They were right about the demand, eventually. But they were catastrophically wrong about the timing. When the bubble burst, 90% of the fiber went dark. Twenty-three telecom companies went bankrupt. Half a million people lost their jobs. The Dow Jones communication technology index dropped 86%. About \$2 trillion in market capitalization vanished.
But here's the twist that makes the story complicated: that "wasted" infrastructure became the physical foundation the modern internet was built on. YouTube, Netflix, Facebook, the entire streaming economy, all of it ran on fiber that was laid by companies that went bankrupt. The overcapacity took a decade to absorb, but it eventually underpinned a transformation far bigger than anyone in 1999 imagined.
The AI infrastructure buildout follows a strikingly similar pattern. Massive capital expenditure that current adoption rates struggle to justify, concentrated in a handful of companies, fueled by the conviction that something enormous is coming even if nobody can say exactly what it looks like.
## The Jevons paradox argument
The bull case for all this spending rests heavily on a 160-year-old economics concept. In 1865, British economist William Stanley Jevons discovered that improvements in coal engine efficiency actually increased total coal consumption, not decreased it. Make a resource cheaper to use and people use more of it, not less.
When DeepSeek revealed that its R1 reasoning model could rival leading AI models at a fraction of the cost, Nvidia's share price dropped 17% in a single day, wiping out roughly \$600 billion in market value. The logic seemed straightforward: if AI models get cheaper, you need fewer GPUs.
Satya Nadella responded by posting the Jevons paradox Wikipedia page on social media. "As AI gets more efficient and accessible," he wrote, "we will see its use skyrocket, turning it into a commodity we just can't get enough of."
The data partially supports this. The inference market grew from virtually nothing to \$106 billion by 2025, with a \$255 billion forecast by 2030. Cheaper tokens don't reduce spending, they unlock new use cases. Every employee with an AI assistant, every automated workflow, every agent pipeline generates tokens that land on GPU infrastructure. Gartner predicts over 90% cost reduction in frontier model inference by 2030, and simultaneously predicts that 40% of AI agent projects will be cancelled due to cost overruns. Both forecasts are coherent. Cheaper inference enables more ambitious projects, and more ambitious projects routinely underestimate their own consumption.
But Jevons paradox has limits. It describes what happens when demand is elastic, when cheaper access genuinely unlocks new applications. The question is whether enterprise AI demand is actually that elastic right now, or whether we're still in the phase where most companies are experimenting without clear ROI.
## But who is the customer?
This is where the story gets uncomfortable. Enterprise AI adoption is still early, and the revenue numbers are murkier than they appear.
BCG's 2025 research found that only 5% of companies are achieving AI value at scale, while 60% report minimal to no material value despite substantial investment. A 2026 survey found that 79% of organizations face challenges in adopting AI, a double-digit increase from the prior year, with 54% of C-suite executives admitting that adopting AI is "tearing their company apart." Cast AI found that most companies underutilize GPUs at just 5% of total capacity, with firms overbuying out of FOMO rather than demand.
Then there's the circular financing problem. Bloomberg has documented the web of interlinked investments where suppliers, customers, and investors overlap. Microsoft invests \$13 billion in OpenAI, which becomes a large customer of Microsoft's cloud services. Amazon invests up to \$25 billion in Anthropic, which commits to spending over \$100 billion on Amazon's cloud technologies over the next decade. Microsoft and Nvidia invest \$15 billion in Anthropic, which taps their chips and cloud capacity. Cloud credits flow to startups who "pay" for computing services from the same companies that invested in them.
As one Wall Street analysis put it, much of the current "AI revenue" is cloud credits being recycled between Big Tech companies. The immediate question investors are asking: how much of this growth is organic demand from actual end users, and how much is the industry financing its own consumption? If the capital being recycled through the ecosystem dries up before these startups achieve independent profitability, the resulting contraction could ripple through the entire S&P 500.
## The bottleneck nobody talks about
Even if the demand materializes, there's a physical constraint that could throttle everything: power.
Data centers now compete with cities for electricity. The IEA reports that data center electricity consumption surged 17% in 2025, with AI-focused facilities growing even faster. A typical hyperscale data center uses 100 megawatts, as much electricity as 100,000 households. Goldman Sachs Research expects data center power demand to grow about 50% to 92 gigawatts by 2027.
The grid can't keep up. In northern Virginia, the world's largest data center market, a voltage fluctuation in July 2024 triggered the simultaneous disconnection of 60 data centers, forcing a 1,500-megawatt emergency adjustment to prevent cascading outages. Residential electricity prices jumped 7.1% in 2025, more than double the inflation rate. In some areas near data center clusters, wholesale electricity costs are up 267% over five years.
Of the 16 gigawatts of data center capacity in the 2026 pipeline, only 5 gigawatts is actually under construction. The gap between what's been announced and what has steel in the ground is enormous. An estimated 30 to 50% of planned 2026 data centers are expected to slip or cancel entirely. The constraint isn't capital or chips anymore. It's megawatts.
Legislators are starting to respond. Several 2026 proposals aim to protect ratepayers from bearing the cost of data center power demand. If utilities and regulators push back on connecting new facilities, the buildout slows regardless of how much money hyperscalers throw at it.
## What happens to startups when the music stops
The infrastructure spending spree has created an ecosystem of subsidized abundance for AI startups. Google offers up to \$350,000 in cloud credits to early-stage companies. AWS, Azure, and OpenAI all run similar programs. The entire startup ecosystem is built on the assumption that compute will remain cheap and available.
But that assumption is a function of the current spending race. If capex gets cut, or even flattens, subsidized API pricing could disappear overnight. Startups that built their unit economics around below-cost inference would face a sudden repricing.
Meta is already making the trade-off explicit. Mark Zuckerberg told employees the company has "two major cost centers: compute infrastructure and people-oriented things," and with more capital flowing toward AI hardware, there's less available for headcount. Meta cut 8,000 jobs in 2026 to fund its infrastructure expansion, and Zuckerberg declined to rule out further reductions. Across the industry, over 150,000 AI-related layoffs have been tracked in 2026 so far, with projections reaching 265,000 by year-end.
The dynamic is clear: the infrastructure buildout is being financed partly through workforce reductions. Companies are spending because they believe AI will generate more value than the people they're replacing. If that bet is wrong, or if the timing is off by even a few years, the human cost will be enormous.
## The FOMO equilibrium
Perhaps the most honest explanation for the spending is the simplest: every company is spending because they're afraid of not spending.
The European Central Bank's Financial Stability Review noted that AI equity valuations may be driven by fear of missing out. Howard Marks of Oaktree Capital observed that FOMO "tends to have a bad reputation, but it's sometimes a healthy instinct. After all, none of us wants to miss out on a once-in-a-lifetime chance to build the future."
This creates a peculiar equilibrium. No single company can afford to slow down because if AI does become as transformative as promised, being two years behind on infrastructure could be an existential mistake. The cost of being wrong about spending is high. The cost of being wrong about not spending might be higher. So everyone keeps writing checks.
Man Group captured this tension in a February 2026 analysis: "AI technology is transformative and here to stay. But the inflated financial architecture supporting it may be unsustainable." The winners, they argued, will be those who solve AI's fundamental economic problems. The losers will be those who monetized the temporary imbalances of the buildout phase.
## Holding the tension
It would be satisfying to declare this a bubble and move on. Or to declare it justified and stop worrying. But the honest answer is that both things can be true simultaneously.
The telecom parallel suggests that the infrastructure being built today will eventually be used, probably for applications we can't yet imagine. The fiber that went dark in 2001 eventually carried Netflix and Zoom. The data centers being built in 2026 may eventually power AI systems that reshape medicine, science, and daily life in ways that make the current spending look modest.
But the telecom parallel also suggests that the companies doing the building may not be the ones who benefit. The investors and workers caught in the bust faced real consequences that the long-term narrative doesn't erase. Timing matters. Overcapacity matters. And the circular financing structures propping up current revenue numbers are genuinely fragile.
The \$725 billion question isn't really about whether AI is important. It obviously is. The question is whether we're building the right amount of infrastructure, at the right time, financed in the right way. And right now, nobody can answer that with any confidence.
That might be the most unsettling part: the biggest capital allocation decision in corporate history is being made on faith.
