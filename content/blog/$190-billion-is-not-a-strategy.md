Microsoft just reported a record quarter, beating Wall Street on both revenue and earnings. Azure is growing 40%. The AI business is on a \$37 billion run rate. And the stock dropped 5%.
The reason? Microsoft told investors it plans to spend \$190 billion on capital expenditures in 2026. That is more than the GDP of most countries. It is a 61% jump from 2025. And it forces a question that the earnings beat cannot answer: when does spending at this scale stop being a strategy and start being an obligation?
## The numbers are staggering
Microsoft's Q3 FY2026 results were genuinely strong. Revenue hit \$82.9 billion, up 18% year over year. Earnings per share came in at \$4.27, well above the \$4.07 consensus. Azure and other cloud services revenue grew 40%, accelerating from 39% in the prior quarter. The AI business crossed a \$37 billion annualized run rate, up 123% year over year. Copilot now exceeds 20 million paid seats.
These are not the numbers of a company in trouble. They are the numbers of a company that has convinced itself the only way to stay ahead is to outspend everyone else.
Microsoft is not alone. The four major hyperscalers, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta, are now on track to spend up to \$725 billion combined on capital expenditures in 2026. That is roughly \$100 billion more than what they projected just one quarter earlier. Amazon holds at \$200 billion. Google revised its top-end forecast to \$190 billion. Meta raised its guidance to between \$125 billion and \$145 billion.
Bridgewater Associates called this a "more dangerous phase" of the AI boom, marked by exponentially rising investment in physical infrastructure and growing reliance on outside capital.
## The depreciation trap
Capital expenditure is not a one-time event. You build a data center now, and you pay for it over the next five to seven years through depreciation. The Wall Street Journal flagged this as a growing blind spot in Big Tech's financials, noting that just five companies are projected to spend a combined \$3 trillion on property and equipment over the next four years. Much of that equipment, particularly AI chips, gets written down over five or six years, hitting profits long after the initial check clears.
The effects are already visible. Microsoft's gross margin in Q3 fell to 67.6%, the narrowest since 2022, as depreciation costs mounted from the data center buildout. Operating margin guidance for Q4 ticked down to 44%, below analyst expectations. Alphabet's return on invested capital is projected to fall from 51% to roughly 36% by 2030. Microsoft's, according to Visible Alpha forecasts compiled by Reuters, could drop from 95% in 2020 to 36% over the same period.
Meta extended the useful life of its AI equipment to reduce depreciation expense by \$2.3 billion in the first nine months of 2025. That is a real accounting choice with real implications: if the equipment actually depreciates faster than the books say, the write-downs come later and bigger.
The concern is not that these companies cannot afford the spending. They can. The concern is that depreciation creates a years-long drag on earnings that persists whether or not the AI revenue materializes at the scale needed to justify it.
## When everyone spends \$150 billion, nobody wins
The arms race dynamic is the most underappreciated risk in this story. If Microsoft spends \$190 billion, Google spends \$190 billion, Amazon spends \$200 billion, and Meta spends \$145 billion, what competitive advantage does any of them actually gain?
They all end up with more data centers, more GPUs, and higher costs. The infrastructure becomes table stakes, not a differentiator. The pricing power shifts to the chip suppliers and the energy providers, not the cloud platforms.
Google Cloud posted 63% revenue growth in Q1, blowing past the 50% estimate and outpacing Azure's 40%. But Google is also spending just as aggressively, and already signaling that 2027 capex will "significantly increase" from 2026. The finish line keeps moving.
Meanwhile, CNBC reported that free cash flow across the hyperscalers could drop dramatically in 2026, with Amazon projected to turn negative. Longbow Asset Management CEO Jake Dollarhide put it simply: "If you're going to pour all this money into AI, it's going to reduce your free cash flow."
## The FOMO problem
A Cast AI report from April 2026 found that companies are utilizing just 5% of their GPU capacity. Ninety-five percent of reserved compute sits idle. The reason is not demand. It is fear. Companies are hoarding GPUs because lead times are long, releasing unrecoverable capacity feels risky, and nobody wants to be the one caught without enough compute when they need it.
At the hyperscaler level, the same psychology applies. Pulling back on spending now could look like an admission of weakness, especially with OpenAI and Anthropic growing their own revenue ahead of expected IPOs. As the WSJ noted, that gives big tech companies "little incentive to tap the spending brakes."
This is the classic dynamic of an arms race driven by fear rather than demand. Every participant is rational individually, and collectively irrational.
## The Anthropic contrast
Anthropic offers an interesting counterpoint. The company confirmed \$30 billion in annualized run-rate revenue, reportedly surpassing OpenAI, while spending roughly 4x less on model training. Its projected gross margin sits at 50% for 2025, climbing to 77% by 2028, compared to OpenAI's approximately 33%.
Anthropic is not building its own data centers. It is leaner, API-focused, and reliant on partnerships with Microsoft, Google, and Amazon for infrastructure. Whether this model scales indefinitely is an open question, but it demonstrates that massive infrastructure ownership is not the only path to AI revenue.
The comparison raises an uncomfortable question for the hyperscalers: if a company with a fraction of your infrastructure budget can match or exceed your AI revenue, what exactly is the \$190 billion buying?
## The telecom parallel
In the late 1990s, telecom companies invested over \$500 billion globally in fiber optic infrastructure. The thesis was simple: internet traffic was growing exponentially, bandwidth demand would be insatiable, and the companies that laid the most cable would win.
The infrastructure was, in fact, useful. Internet traffic did grow exponentially. But the companies that built the networks mostly went bankrupt. Global Crossing, WorldCom, and dozens of others collapsed under the weight of debt and depreciation. The value accrued to the companies that used the infrastructure, not the ones that built it.
The parallel is not perfect. Today's hyperscalers have vastly stronger balance sheets, massive cash-generating core businesses, and actual customer demand for their AI services. Microsoft's Azure growth is real. The \$37 billion AI run rate is real. But the telecom lesson is not that the technology was wrong. It is that building the infrastructure and profiting from it are two different things.
As one analysis from Fabricated Knowledge noted, the capital intensity of AI infrastructure spending is approaching the per-capita equivalent of the telecom buildout, adjusted for population, roughly \$1.2 trillion. The AI industry may be better capitalized, but the structural risk of overbuilding is the same.
## What \$190 billion actually buys
Microsoft's spending is buying real things. Data centers across the world. GPU clusters from Nvidia. Custom silicon development. Energy infrastructure to power it all. And it is working, in the sense that Azure is growing and AI revenue is accelerating.
But the market's reaction to the Q3 earnings tells you something. Investors are no longer rewarding the spending itself. They want to see the return. Microsoft's stock fell 5% on the day of the earnings beat. Earlier in the year, it fell 10% after Q2 results, wiping \$357 billion in market value, the second-largest single-day decline on record for any company.
The question is not whether AI is transformative. It probably is. The question is whether \$190 billion in a single year is the right amount to spend, or whether it is a number driven by competitive pressure, fear of falling behind, and the institutional momentum of plans set in motion before anyone fully understood the cost.
Satya Nadella told Morgan Stanley that the investment will pay off. Maybe it will. But "trust us, it'll pay off" is exactly what every telecom CEO said in 1999. The infrastructure was useful. The companies that built it often were not.
\$190 billion is not a strategy. It is a bet. And the distinction matters.
