A recruiter spends six seconds on your resume. An AI can generate a flawless one in six seconds too. So what exactly is a resume proving anymore?
We're entering a strange new chapter in hiring. AI can write cover letters that sound human, pass technical assessments, and produce polished portfolios overnight. The traditional signals that employers relied on, degrees, certifications, whiteboard interview performance, are losing their meaning fast. Not because they were never valuable, but because they can now be manufactured at scale with zero effort.
The question isn't whether you're qualified. It's whether anyone can tell.
## The credential collapse
For decades, hiring worked on a simple premise: credentials are hard to get, so they must be meaningful. A computer science degree from a good university? That took years of effort. A well-written resume with strong bullet points? That required real experience to draw from.
AI has blown that apart. Large language models can generate articulate, keyword-optimized resumes tailored to any job description in seconds. They can draft cover letters that hit every emotional beat a recruiter looks for. They can even solve coding challenges that would have filtered out most candidates a few years ago.
The result is what hiring experts are calling a "signal-to-noise crisis." When everyone's application looks polished, polish stops being a signal. Recruiters are drowning in AI-generated applications that all sound the same, and the old filters no longer work.
As one industry analysis put it, "If an AI can write a bullet point, an AI can fake a bullet point." The shift now is from screening for keywords to verifying human potential.
## Proof of work beats proof of credentials
So if credentials can be faked, what can't be?
A body of shipped work.
Think about it: an AI can generate a resume that says you built a SaaS product. But it can't generate the Git history, the iteration logs, the user feedback, the blog posts documenting what you learned, the public evidence that you actually did the thing over weeks and months.
This is the "proof of work" principle applied to careers. Every commit, every blog post, every shipped feature is a timestamp of taste, judgment, and follow-through. It's not about building something impressive once. It's about showing up consistently and making things that exist in the world.
A portfolio of shipped, public, maintained projects tells a story that no resume can. It shows:
- **Taste** , because you chose what to build and how to design it
- **Judgment** , because you made tradeoffs and scoped decisions
- **Follow-through** , because you finished it and put it out there
- **Consistency** , because you kept going when nobody was watching
This isn't new advice. Developers have been told to "build projects" for years. But AI makes it urgent. When the floor for application quality rises to near-perfection, the only way to stand above it is with evidence that can't be generated on demand.
## Consistency over intensity
There's a temptation to think this means you need to build something massive. A startup. A framework. An open-source project with thousands of stars.
You don't.
What matters more is consistency. A blog with a hundred posts written over two years says more about you than a single weekend hackathon project. A small utility tool that you've maintained and updated for six months shows more than a flashy demo you built in a day.
The compounding effect of showing up daily to build, publish, and ship is something credentials can never replicate. It's the career equivalent of compound interest, and it works in ways that a degree simply doesn't.
Pick one thing. Ship it publicly. Keep going. The bar isn't "build a startup." It's "show me you can finish something."
## The Singapore angle
This matters especially in Singapore's current landscape. Software developers ranked as the third most sought-after professionals in 2025, and demand for AI and digital skills continues to climb. The Ministry of Manpower's data shows that PMET roles, particularly in tech, account for more than half of all job openings.
But here's the thing: Singapore's tech market is also fiercely competitive. With top-tier universities producing strong graduates and global companies setting up regional headquarters here, the talent pool is deep. A degree from NUS or NTU is table stakes, not a differentiator.
What's encouraging is that Singapore's employers are increasingly moving toward skills-based hiring. That's a shift away from pedigree and toward demonstrated capability. For local developers, this is an opportunity. A well-maintained GitHub profile, a personal blog documenting your learning, or an open-source contribution that solves a real problem can set you apart in ways that another line on your resume cannot.
## The privilege question
I want to be honest about something: not everyone has the luxury of side projects. If you're working long hours, managing caregiving responsibilities, or just trying to get by, the idea of "building in public" can feel tone-deaf.
That's a fair criticism, and I don't want to dismiss it.
But the argument isn't that everyone needs a massive portfolio. It's that *some* shipped work beats *any* amount of credentials. Even small, consistent output matters. A single well-documented project. A handful of thoughtful blog posts. A contribution to an existing open-source tool.
The bar is lower than most people think. You don't need to build something that goes viral. You need to build something that *exists*, that someone can look at and say, "This person can finish things."
## Formal education still has value
I'm not here to tell you your degree is worthless. It's not. Education builds foundational knowledge, teaches you how to think, and opens doors that might otherwise stay closed.
But as a standalone signal, it's weakening. A degree tells me you can learn. A shipped project tells me you can do. In a world where AI handles more and more of the "learning" and "knowing" parts of work, the "doing" part becomes the scarce resource.
The strongest position is both: formal education *plus* a body of work. But if you had to choose one to invest your next hundred hours in, I'd pick the project every time.
## What to do right now
If you're convinced but unsure where to start, here's a simple playbook:
1. **Pick one format.** Blog, open-source tool, side product, newsletter. Don't try to do everything.
2. **Make it public.** The whole point is that it's verifiable. Private repos and unpublished drafts don't count.
3. **Ship something small first.** A single blog post. A tiny CLI tool. A one-page website. Momentum matters more than ambition.
4. **Keep going.** Set a cadence, weekly, biweekly, whatever you can sustain, and stick to it. Consistency is the signal.
5. **Document your thinking.** Don't just show the output. Show the process. Write about why you made the choices you made. That's where taste becomes visible.
The world is getting noisier. AI is raising the floor on what "good enough" looks like. But it can't fake a track record. It can't fake years of showing up. It can't fake the messy, human reality of building something from nothing and putting it out into the world.
Your side project is your resume. Start building it.
