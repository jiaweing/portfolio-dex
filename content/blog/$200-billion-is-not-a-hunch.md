When Amazon CEO Andy Jassy told shareholders that the company would spend roughly \$200 billion in capital expenditures this year, mostly on AI infrastructure, he was careful to add a qualifier: "This isn't some sort of quixotic, top-line grab." In other words, it's not a hunch.
That number is worth sitting with. \$200 billion is larger than the GDP of most countries. It's more than Amazon's entire net income in 2025. And it's just one company's line item in what's shaping up to be a \$650 to \$700 billion spending year across the four major hyperscalers.
The interesting question isn't whether this is reckless. It's why companies like Amazon can make this bet at all, and what it means for everyone who can't.
## The math behind the conviction
Jassy's confidence isn't really about believing AI will work. It's about the asymmetry of the bet.
Amazon Web Services ended 2025 with a \$142 billion annual revenue run rate, growing 24% year over year. The company's custom chip business, including Graviton, Trainium, and Nitro, has crossed \$20 billion in annualized revenue and is growing at triple-digit percentages. Even with \$200 billion in capex, Amazon turned \$717 billion in total revenue into \$80 billion of operating income last year.
The logic isn't "AI will definitely be transformative." The logic is: if AI wins, Amazon needs the infrastructure to capture that demand. If AI disappoints, Amazon is still AWS, still the world's largest cloud provider, still sitting on data center capacity that other workloads will happily consume. The downside is margin pressure. The upside is generational dominance.
This is survivorship math, not conviction. And it's a kind of math that only works when you're already enormous.
## An arms race where sitting out is the real risk
Amazon isn't spending in isolation. Alphabet is projecting over \$50 billion in 2026 capex to support Gemini. Microsoft poured \$14 billion into cloud and AI infrastructure in Q4 2025 alone. Meta guided toward \$38 to \$40 billion for 2025, with plans to scale further.
Goldman Sachs Research has pointed out that consensus capex estimates have been too low for two straight years. At the start of both 2024 and 2025, analysts expected roughly 20% capex growth. Actual growth exceeded 50% both times. And even at these levels, AI-related capex sits at about 0.8% of GDP, well below the 1.5% peak reached during previous technology investment cycles like the late-1990s telecom buildout.
Morgan Stanley has gone further, warning that a transformative leap in AI capabilities is imminent and that most of the world isn't positioned for it. Their research estimates nearly \$3 trillion of AI-related infrastructure investment will flow through the global economy by 2028, with more than 80% of that spending still ahead.
The implication is stark: the companies that don't build now risk being locked out of the next platform shift entirely. In an arms race this large, the biggest risk isn't overspending. It's sitting out.
## Cheaper AI means more spending, not less
Jassy made an interesting claim in his shareholder letter: AI will get "much cheaper over time." He's probably right. But cheaper doesn't mean less money spent. If anything, the opposite.
This is Jevons paradox playing out in real time. When the British economist William Stanley Jevons studied coal consumption in the 1860s, he found that more efficient steam engines didn't reduce coal use. They made coal so affordable that consumption tripled. The same dynamic is emerging in AI.
When DeepSeek demonstrated that competitive AI models could be trained more cheaply, Nvidia's stock initially dropped 17% in a single day, wiping out \$600 billion in market value. The fear was logical: cheaper models should mean less demand for chips and compute.
But the opposite happened. Cheaper models lowered barriers to entry, more companies started training and deploying AI, inference volumes exploded, and total spending climbed. Per-unit costs fell while aggregate spend rose. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella explicitly invoked Jevons paradox, writing on social media that "as AI gets more efficient and accessible, we will see its use skyrocket."
This is precisely why the capex numbers keep exceeding expectations. Every efficiency gain unlocks new demand. The ceiling keeps moving.
## The moat isn't the model
There's a tempting narrative that the AI race will be won by whoever builds the best model. But Amazon's bet suggests a different thesis: the moat isn't the model. It's the infrastructure and distribution.
AWS doesn't need to build the best foundation model in the world. It needs to be the place where everyone else runs theirs. Amazon's Bedrock marketplace already offers third-party models alongside its own Nova models. The strategy is platform, not product.
And the distribution advantage extends far beyond cloud. Amazon has hardware in hundreds of millions of homes through Alexa, Ring, and Kindle. It has logistics infrastructure that reaches the last mile. It has a payments ecosystem through Prime. Each of these is a surface where AI can be embedded and monetized.
Jassy noted in his letter that Amazon is "monetizing capacity as fast as we can install it," and that the company faces capacity constraints resulting in unserved demand. When your bottleneck is that you can't build data centers fast enough to meet customer orders, spending \$200 billion starts to look less like a gamble and more like fulfilling backlog.
## What this means for startups
Here's where the picture gets uncomfortable for everyone outside the hyperscaler club.
Anthropic, arguably the most well-funded AI startup in history, raised \$30 billion in its Series G round in February 2026, valuing the company at \$380 billion. Its total funding to date stands at roughly \$67 billion. That sounds enormous until you realize it's about a third of what Amazon plans to spend in a single year.
Anthropic's annualized revenue run rate is reportedly \$14 billion, growing at impressive rates. But the company is still burning cash, with break-even not expected until 2028. Compare that to Amazon, which generated \$80 billion in operating income while planning to spend \$200 billion on capex.
The asymmetry is structural. Hyperscalers can fund AI infrastructure from existing cash flows. They can absorb years of compressed margins because their core businesses generate enough revenue to subsidize the buildout. Startups, no matter how well-funded, are drawing down finite capital.
This doesn't mean startups can't win. It means they can't win the infrastructure game. The path forward for smaller players is to build on top of the infrastructure the giants are providing, to find wedges where distribution advantages don't apply, and to move faster in vertical markets where incumbents are too broad to specialize.
But if your strategy requires out-spending Amazon on compute, you've already lost.
## The downside nobody wants to talk about
None of this means the \$200 billion bet is risk-free.
Amazon's free cash flow dropped from \$32.9 billion to \$7.7 billion as capital spending consumed 94.5% of operating cash flow. Analysts project Amazon could turn free-cash-flow negative in 2026. Across the hyperscalers, free cash flow could decline by up to 90% this year.
Shareholder patience has limits. When Amazon announced the \$200 billion figure, its stock dropped immediately. Investors are comfortable with growth spending, but there's a line between investment and excess, and \$200 billion is testing where that line sits.
Morgan Stanley has drawn explicit parallels to the dot-com era's telecom investment boom. That cycle ended badly for many participants. The AI buildout may be more justified, the demand signals are arguably clearer, but the scale of spending is approaching territory where even strong demand may not fully justify the capital deployed.
The question isn't whether AI infrastructure will generate returns. It almost certainly will. The question is whether \$200 billion in a single year generates enough return fast enough to satisfy the people writing the checks.
## The takeaway
Jassy is right that this isn't a hunch. The demand data, the revenue growth, the competitive dynamics, they all point in the same direction. But "not a hunch" and "guaranteed to work" are very different statements.
What Amazon is really saying is that the cost of being wrong about AI is manageable, while the cost of being right and unprepared is catastrophic. That calculus only works when you have the balance sheet to absorb the downside and the existing business to fund the upside.
For the rest of the market, the message is simpler: the infrastructure layer of AI is being claimed by a handful of companies willing to spend more than most nations. Whether that concentration of compute becomes the defining advantage of the next decade depends on how much value the AI running on top of it actually creates.
We're about to find out.
