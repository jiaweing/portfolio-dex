I've been thinking about how YouTube feels different now, and I think the best way to explain it is through a design analogy.
## The flat design moment
Back in 2013, Apple made one of the most consequential design decisions in tech history. With iOS 7, Jony Ive ripped out every trace of skeuomorphism, the leather textures, the glossy buttons, the felt-green Game Center, and replaced it all with flat, minimal surfaces. It was polarizing. Some designers mourned the loss of craft and depth. Others celebrated the clean simplicity.
But here's the thing that mattered more than whether it was "good" or "bad": it changed *everything*. The entire industry followed. Google refined Material Design. Microsoft doubled down on Metro. Within a couple of years, every app, every website, every interface looked like a variation of the same flat, colorful grid. The visual language of the internet got flattened into a single paradigm.
And now, with iOS 26, Apple is swinging back again with "Liquid Glass," a translucent, layered aesthetic that borrows from skeuomorphism's depth while keeping flat design's simplicity. The cycle continues, but the pattern is clear: once a dominant player optimizes in a direction, the rest of the industry follows until everything looks the same.
YouTube is in its flat design era right now. And it's suffocating.
## The MrBeast blueprint
MrBeast didn't just become the biggest YouTuber in the world. He reverse-engineered the platform. Every thumbnail is A/B tested. Every title is optimized for click-through rate. The pacing of his videos is calculated to maximize retention curves. The first few seconds hook you, the middle keeps escalating, and the payoff lands right before you'd think about clicking away.
It works. He has over 443 million subscribers. But the problem isn't that MrBeast does this. The problem is that his success created a template that the entire platform now treats as the *only* way to succeed. Just like flat design became the default after Apple adopted it, the MrBeast format became the default for YouTube.
Researchers have noted how platform algorithms don't just recommend content, they shape what gets created in the first place. When YouTube's recommendation system rewards a specific format, creators adapt. Not because they want to, but because the alternative is invisibility.
## Everything is the same now
Open YouTube right now and scroll through your recommendations. You'll notice patterns everywhere:
- Videos are almost always around 10 minutes (long enough to maximize ad revenue, short enough to retain viewers)
- Thumbnails follow the same formula: big face, bright colors, exaggerated expression, minimal text
- Titles use the same emotional triggers: "I Tried...", "This Changed Everything", "You Won't Believe..."
- The narrative arc is identical: hook, escalation, payoff, call to action
The storylines blend together. The editing styles are interchangeable. Even creators in completely different niches, cooking, tech, travel, gaming, end up producing videos that *feel* the same because they're all optimizing for the same metrics.
YouTube Shorts made this even worse. The short-form format is so aggressively optimized for retention that every video competes in a pure attention lottery. There's no room for slow burns, for weirdness, for the kind of content that doesn't immediately grab you but stays with you for days.
## Where the soul went
I remember when YouTube was fun. Not "optimized for engagement" fun, but genuinely, unpredictably fun. People uploaded videos because they had something to say or show, not because they'd reverse-engineered the algorithm's preferences.
The early YouTube era had a rawness to it. Creators talked to cameras in their bedrooms with terrible lighting. Videos were as long or as short as they needed to be. There was no "meta" to follow, no format bible, no retention graph to worship. The content was unpolished, and that's exactly what made it feel human.
Now YouTube is a business. And I don't mean that in the vague, hand-wavy sense. I mean it in the literal, operational sense: creators hire editors, thumbnail designers, title consultants, and analytics teams. They run their channels like media companies because the platform demands it. If you don't, you're competing against people who do, and the algorithm will bury you.
Creator burnout is a well-documented consequence of this shift. The pressure to constantly produce, optimize, and perform takes a real psychological toll. When every video is a calculated bet against an opaque algorithm, the creative joy gets squeezed out.
## The optimization trap
This is the core of the problem. Optimization isn't inherently bad. Good editing, clear titles, and engaging thumbnails are all improvements. But there's a tipping point where optimization stops serving the content and starts replacing it. When every creative decision is filtered through "will this perform?", you end up with content that's technically perfect and emotionally hollow.
It's the same thing that happened with flat design. The original insight, that interfaces should be cleaner and less cluttered, was valid. But when the entire industry optimized toward the same minimal aesthetic, we lost texture, personality, and visual diversity. Everything became "clean" in the same way. The design world had to course-correct.
YouTube hasn't course-corrected yet. If anything, it's doubling down. The algorithm keeps rewarding the same patterns, creators keep following them, and viewers keep watching because there's nothing else in their feed.
## What I actually miss
I miss the randomness. I miss stumbling onto a video that breaks every "rule" of YouTube and being captivated anyway. I miss creators who were clearly making things for themselves first and an audience second. I miss the feeling that YouTube was a place where weird, niche, deeply personal content could find its people without being optimized into oblivion.
Maybe the pendulum will swing back, the way Apple eventually swung back from pure flat design to something with more depth and texture. Maybe a new generation of creators will reject the template and do something different. But right now, YouTube feels like it's stuck in its flat design era: clean, efficient, optimized, and missing something essential.
The soul isn't gone. It's just buried under a mountain of retention graphs.
