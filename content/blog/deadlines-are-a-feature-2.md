We treat deadlines as stress. As constraints to rebel against, or at least resent. But what if deadlines aren't the enemy of good work? What if they're the reason good work exists at all?
The more I think about it, the more I believe deadlines aren't a necessary evil. They're a feature of how humans actually get things done.
## Parkinson's law is not a joke
In 1955, British historian Cyril Northcote Parkinson opened an essay in *The Economist* with a deceptively simple observation: "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." It was originally written as satire about bureaucratic inefficiency, but it stuck because everyone recognized the truth in it.
Give yourself two weeks to write a proposal that could take two days, and you'll spend the first ten days "thinking about it," reorganizing your notes, and doing research you don't need. The proposal doesn't get better with more time. It just takes longer.
Despite its humorous origins, Parkinson's Law has been widely adopted in management science and social psychology. Research supports the core premise: people anchor on available time rather than required time. A task that could take two hours genuinely stretches to fill a full day when that's what's on the calendar.
The fix isn't to work harder. It's to give yourself less time.
## Runway is just a deadline with consequences
Startups ship faster than big companies. This is not controversial. But the usual explanation, that startups are "more agile" or have "less bureaucracy," misses something important.
Startups ship faster because they're running out of money.
Runway is a deadline. A real one. When you have 18 months of cash and a product that doesn't exist yet, every week matters. There's no time for a six-month planning cycle or a committee to review the committee's recommendations. You build, you ship, you learn, or you die.
Big companies don't have that constraint. A task that takes two months in a startup might take six months or more in a large organization, not because the work is harder, but because there's no existential pressure to finish it. The bureaucracy isn't the root cause. The absence of a real deadline is.
This is why the best leaders inside large organizations create artificial constraints that mimic startup urgency. Not fake deadlines with no consequences, but real commitments tied to launches, customers, or public announcements.
## Deadlines are a prioritization engine
Here's what most people miss about deadlines: they don't just make you faster. They make you smarter about what matters.
When time is unlimited, everything feels important. You can polish the deck, tweak the copy, add one more feature, run one more analysis. But when the clock is ticking, you're forced to ask a different question: *What actually needs to happen for this to ship?*
That compression is clarifying. It strips away the nice-to-haves and forces you to focus on the essential. You can't do everything, so you do the right thing.
I've experienced this firsthand. Publishing on a regular cadence means every post has a deadline. That constraint doesn't make the writing worse. It makes it more focused. Capstone projects in school worked the same way. The tightest timelines produced the clearest thinking, because there was no room for overthinking.
## The "I work better without pressure" myth
Some people genuinely believe they perform best without deadlines. In most cases, what they actually mean is they prefer to procrastinate without guilt.
Research from Dan Ariely and Klaus Wertenbroch at MIT and INSEAD found that people readily self-impose deadlines to combat procrastination, and that these deadlines do help improve task performance. The catch? Self-imposed deadlines aren't as effective as externally imposed ones. People don't set them optimally, they give themselves too much slack.
This makes sense. Without external pressure, you'll always negotiate with yourself. "I'll start tomorrow" is the most repeated lie in human productivity. The people who consistently ship aren't the ones who found the perfect pressure-free environment. They're the ones who built deadlines into their process, whether through accountability partners, public commitments, or simple routines.
## AI makes deadlines more achievable, not less necessary
With AI tools accelerating research, drafting, and iteration, you might think deadlines matter less now. If you can do in two hours what used to take two days, why not relax the timeline?
The opposite is true. AI compresses the *execution* part of work, which means the bottleneck shifts entirely to *decision-making and shipping*. You can draft faster, but you still need to decide what to write. You can generate options faster, but you still need to pick one and commit.
Without a deadline, AI tools just give you more time to tinker. More drafts, more variations, more "let me try one more thing." The work expands to fill the time, just like Parkinson predicted, except now there's even more work you *could* do.
Deadlines remain the forcing function. AI changes what's possible within the constraint. It doesn't remove the need for the constraint itself.
## The danger of fake deadlines
Not all deadlines are created equal. The difference between a useful deadline and a harmful one comes down to consequences.
Real deadlines have real stakes. A product launch date announced to customers. A grant submission window that closes. A publishing schedule your readers expect you to keep. Miss these, and something tangible happens.
Fake deadlines, the ones managers set arbitrarily to "create urgency," don't work. Everyone sees through them. When a team learns that "end of week" really means "whenever," the deadline loses all power. Worse, fake deadlines breed cynicism. People stop treating any deadline as real, even the ones that matter.
The solution isn't to eliminate deadlines. It's to make them honest. Attach them to real commitments. Make the consequences clear. And if the deadline genuinely doesn't matter, don't pretend it does.
## Consistency is just a daily deadline
There's a popular idea that "consistency beats intensity." Show up every day, do the work, and results compound over time. It's good advice, but it obscures the mechanism.
Consistency *is* a deadline. A daily publishing schedule means today's post is due today. A weekly review means the review happens this week. The rhythm creates a recurring constraint that prevents work from drifting indefinitely into the future.
This is why daily habits are so powerful. Not because there's anything magical about doing something every day, but because "today" is the shortest possible deadline. There's no room for Parkinson's Law when the time available is measured in hours.
## Deadlines aren't the villain
None of this is an argument for crunch culture. Deadlines imposed without adequate resources, realistic scope, or genuine input from the people doing the work aren't deadlines. They're traps. There's a meaningful difference between "we need to ship by Friday because customers are waiting" and "we need to ship by Friday because someone put a date on a slide."
The best deadlines are honest, achievable, and tied to something that matters. They respect the people doing the work while refusing to let the work expand forever.
Deadlines get a bad reputation because bad deadlines are genuinely toxic. But the answer isn't to remove all time constraints. It's to create better ones.
Because without a deadline, nothing ships. And that's not a bug in human nature. It's the feature.
